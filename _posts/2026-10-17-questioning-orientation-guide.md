### A guide for anyone questioning their sexual orientation

*For people who don't know yet — and for those who suspect something but aren't ready to name it.
Written to be useful whether you're 18 or 40, certain of nothing, and tired of being told to "just
figure it out".*

> **Two promises up front.**
> 1. **This guide will not tell you what you are.** Nobody outside your head can do that, and anyone
>    who claims to is selling something.
> 2. **Not knowing is a legitimate place to be.** Uncertainty is not a stage you must fail out of.
>    Some people settle on a label in a week; some take a decade; some never use one. All fine.

---

## 1. What orientation actually is

Most confusion comes from squeezing four separable things into one word:

| Dimension | The question it answers | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Attraction** | Who do I feel drawn to? | "I notice women." |
| **Behaviour** | What have I actually done? | "I've only dated men." |
| **Identity** | What do I call myself? | "Lesbian." "Bi." "Still figuring it out." |
| **Romance** | Who do I want to build a life with? | "I want a partner, not sex." |

These can point in different directions, and that is not hypocrisy or confusion. Someone can be
attracted to multiple genders, have only dated one, call themselves "queer", and prefer emotional
intimacy over sex. Every one of those is a real, common configuration.

Two consequences worth internalising:

- **Behaviour is a weak test of orientation.** Plenty of gay people have heterosexual histories;
  plenty of straight people have had same-sex experiences. What you've done doesn't determine what
  you are.
- **Sexual and romantic attraction can differ.** The "split attraction model" — sexual attraction and
  romantic attraction as separate axes — is genuinely useful for people who realise they want
  companionship but not sex, or vice versa. It's a tool, not a doctrine.

## 2. Things almost everyone questioning feels

If you recognise yourself here, you are not uniquely broken:

- **"Am I faking it?"** — the most common thought of all, in both directions. If you were faking, you'd
  know; faking is deliberate. Ambivalence is not fraud.
- **Retroactive rereading.** Suddenly reinterpreting old friendships, crushes, or "intense" adolescent
  attachments. Normal, and often unreliable in both directions.
- **Hypervigilance.** Noticing every attractive person and treating it as evidence for a trial.
  Exhausting, and it distorts what you observe.
- **Grief.** For an imagined future — the wedding, the grandchildren, the easy life. Real and worth
  naming, even if nothing bad has happened.
- **Relief and terror at once.** Very common.
- **Wanting someone to just tell you.** Also very common, and exactly the wish that makes people
  vulnerable to bad advice.

## 3. Ten myths about questioning

| Myth | Why it's wrong |
|---|---|
| "If you're confused, you're probably gay." | Confusion is compatible with any outcome, including straight. |
| "You can't know without sleeping with someone." | Most people know from attraction and longing, not experience. Experimenting is optional, not a prerequisite. |
| "Bisexuality is a phase / a stopover." | For many people it is a stable, lifelong orientation. |
| "You have to pick a side eventually." | You don't. Unlabelled, queer, and "no label" are all legitimate. |
| "If you've had straight relationships, you're just experimenting." | History doesn't invalidate current attraction. |
| "Being attracted to one person of the same sex doesn't count." | It counts as information. Whether it makes you "something" is your call. |
| "Asexuality means you're broken or repressed." | Asexuality is an orientation, not a symptom. |
| "You need to come out to be authentic." | Privacy is not inauthenticity. Timing is yours. |
| "Questioning is just internalised homophobia." | Sometimes stigma is in the mix — but the questioning itself is legitimate. |
| "Once you decide, it's permanent." | Identities can shift. You're allowed to revise. |

## 4. A self-reflection toolkit

Not a test. No scoring. Just prompts that tend to surface useful information. Write, don't ruminate.

**The attraction audit**
- Over the past year, whose attention did you want? Not who was available — who you *wanted*.
- When you imagine a future partnership, who is in the room?
- Which fictional characters or celebrities have you been drawn to, and what did you want from them —
  to be them, or to be with them? (The "want to be / want to be with" confusion is extremely common and
  especially relevant if you're also questioning gender.)
- Do you feel attraction *toward* people, or mainly the wish to be *chosen* by them? Those are different.

**The avoidance test**
- Is there a category of person you consistently avoid thinking about? Avoidance is data.
- What would you do if you were certain no one would ever find out? (Then ask: what's actually stopping
  you — safety, or shame?)

**The cost test**
- When you imagine being straight, what do you feel? Relief? Disappointment? Nothing?
- When you imagine being gay/bi, what do you feel? Fear of consequences, or fear of the identity itself?
  Those need different responses.

**The company test**
- Whose company feels like rest? Notice the people you don't have to perform around.

## 5. Exploring, in order of risk

Work down this ladder at whatever pace is safe. You don't have to do the later rungs at all.

**Rung 1 — Private and unobservable**
Read memoirs, essays, forums, wikis. Watch films with queer characters and notice your reactions.
Journal. Nothing here is visible to anyone else.

**Rung 2 — Anonymous**
Online communities under a pseudonym, advice forums, helplines. Use a separate account, and assume
nothing on the internet is truly anonymous — don't post identifying details, photos, or your school.

**Rung 3 — One trusted person**
A friend, an older sibling, a counsellor, a cousin who lives far away. The first disclosure is the
hardest; it gets easier. Choose someone whose reaction you can survive.

**Rung 4 — Semi-public**
A student group, a meetup, a support circle, a therapist experienced with sexuality. Exposure without
announcement.

**Rung 5 — Dating or intimacy**
Optional. Apps and dating carry privacy risk (screenshots, outing, scams). Use photos that aren't on
your other accounts, meet in public, tell someone where you are. Nobody owes anyone an experiment.

**Privacy checklist before any online step:** separate email and handle · no face photo in a
recognisable location · location sharing off · check who can see your contacts · assume a screenshot
can circulate.

## 6. Labels: tools, not contracts

- **Use one when it helps** — they connect you to people, language and information.
- **Don't use one when it doesn't.** "I don't know", "queer", "unlabelled" are complete answers.
- **Expect revision.** People move between labels, and that's normal rather than evidence of dishonesty.
- **Beware label pressure from inside the community too.** Some spaces police bisexuality, asexuality
  or "not gay enough". You don't owe anyone a purity test.

---

## 7. "Am I faking it?" and the voice that isn't yours

Two things can be true at once: your uncertainty is genuine, and you've absorbed messages that make
certain answers feel dangerous. It's worth separating them.

- **Minority stress** — the chronic vigilance that comes from expecting rejection — produces exactly the
  symptoms people misread as "not really gay": numbness, avoidance, self-doubt.
- **Compulsory heterosexuality** — the assumption that straight is the default and anything else needs
  justification — makes questioning feel like an accusation. Useful as a lens; unhelpful as a
  verdict, because it can't tell you what your own answer is.
- **Internalised stigma** usually sounds like a moral judgement ("this is wrong") rather than a
  factual uncertainty ("I don't know"). If your doubt has a moral tone, ask whose voice it is in.

## 8. Telling people: a decision framework

Ask yourself five questions before any disclosure:

1. **Safety** — could this person respond with violence, expulsion from housing, or loss of funding?
2. **Dependence** — do I rely on them financially or for accommodation?
3. **Reversibility** — can I un-say this? (Usually no. Assume it spreads.)
4. **Support** — if it goes badly, who do I have afterwards?
5. **Timing** — is there a reason to do it now, or is it pressure?

If any of 1–3 is alarming, wait. Waiting is not cowardice; it's strategy.

**Staged disclosure** works: tell one person, see how it feels, adjust. You are allowed to tell someone
and then ask them not to tell anyone else — and to be explicit about that.

**If it goes badly:** you are not obliged to manage their reaction. Give them time, but don't debate
your existence. Have somewhere to go afterwards. Remember that a bad first disclosure is not a verdict
on your future.

## 9. Red flags

- **Anyone offering a "cure", "conversion", "reparative therapy" or "corrective" counselling.** These
  have no evidence base, are opposed by major professional bodies, and are associated with depression
  and suicidality. In China, courts have repeatedly ruled against providers.
- **A therapist who tells you your orientation before you do.** A good one helps you explore; a bad one
  assigns a verdict.
- **"Just try it with me"** — coercion dressed as help.
- **Partners or friends who threaten to out you** to control you. That is abuse, regardless of context.
- **Any group that demands you cut off family or friends as a price of belonging.**
- **Online "tests"** that claim to score your orientation. They measure nothing.

## 10. When to get help — and for what

Distress does not necessarily mean your orientation is the problem. It often means **stigma, isolation
or family conflict** is. Useful distinctions:

- **Distress about *being* something** → usually needs acceptance and community, not diagnosis.
- **Distress about *what others will do*** → needs practical support and safety planning.
- **Persistent low mood, self-harm, or feeling you can't go on** → needs clinical help, urgently.
  This is not about orientation; it's about treatable suffering.

Campus counselling centres are usually confidential under professional ethics codes, with exceptions
for imminent safety risk — worth reading their confidentiality page before you go. General hotlines
(verify current numbers): 400-161-9995 (national 24-hour line), 010-82951332 (Beijing crisis centre).

## 11. If you're also questioning gender

Orientation and gender are separate axes, but they get tangled in practice. Two common patterns:

- **"I'm attracted to men — so maybe I'm a gay man"** when actually the wish is to be *seen as* a man,
  or to be desired *as* a man.
- **"I want to be a woman, so I must be attracted to men"** — not necessarily.

A rough sorting question: when you imagine the person you want, is the emphasis on **who they are**, or
on **who you would be with them**? If it's mostly the latter, gender may be the live question rather
than orientation. You can explore both, separately, without resolving either yet.

## 12. Notes for readers in mainland China

- **Family pressure is the dominant variable**, not campus culture: 催婚, 相亲, and expectations about
  grandchildren arrive earlier than in many other countries' university systems.
- **形婚 (cooperative marriage of convenience)** is sometimes proposed as a solution. It carries serious
  legal, financial and emotional risk — no legal protection, possible fraud exposure, and often
  compounding pressure later. Treat it as a last resort, never a plan.
- **Campus environments vary enormously.** Some are relaxed; some have parent chat groups, mandatory
  check-ins or counsellors who contact families. Check before disclosing to staff (see the
  [student guide](lgbt-college-guide-china-en.md)).
- **Online information is filtered and unstable.** Save anything you rely on — medical, legal, community
  contacts — locally.
- **Community resources have contracted** in recent years; several long-running organisations have
  closed. Verify that a group still exists before trusting it with anything sensitive.
- **The vocabulary is contested.** You may encounter outdated clinical terms (易性癖, 性身份异常) in
  textbooks or from doctors. They describe an old framework, not you.

## 13. If you want a structure

Not a programme, just a gentle shape for six weeks:

| Week | Do one thing |
|---|---|
| 1 | Journal on the four dimensions in §1. No conclusions. |
| 2 | Read one first-person account that isn't a tragedy. |
| 3 | Find one anonymous community and lurk only. |
| 4 | Tell one safe person, or decide deliberately not to. |
| 5 | Write down what you'd want your life to look like in five years — without naming an orientation. |
| 6 | Re-read week 1 and week 5. Notice what changed. Change nothing on purpose. |

**One last thing.** The goal of this process is not to arrive at a label. It's to stop spending energy
on vigilance and start spending it on your life. If a label helps you do that, take one. If it doesn't,
leave it on the shelf. Either way, you don't have to decide today.
